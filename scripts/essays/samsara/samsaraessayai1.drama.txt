The exam.
How do you currently feel about samsara? Lock in your best current idea and register how sure you feel. Now take the honest position: I do not claim to know what samsara is. Imagine this is an exam and you had to write something. What are you writing? Pause. Now you have thought about it — are you more or less sure you know what it is? Likely less, which is good. Thinking you know something is the main blocker to actually knowing something. Not truly understanding it, holding ideas that feel familiar enough to be acceptable, is more dangerous than knowing you know less.

[pause, section change]

Where questions come from.
We could ask for the right answer, but the real process is revaluating every held belief the new information conflicts with. When a conflict arises, a question emerges. A question is the child of two conflicting viewpoints. Why is the sky blue? You do not see the previous process that birthed it: the sky is blue, there is no obvious reason the sky is blue, thus the great why emerges as the solvent for all confusion. Where would we be without why? You could be like Patrick Star and have no questions, because you have not found your own lack of understanding. Let us make that clear: you do not see your own lack of understanding. This is the whole foundation, and the impossible thing to grasp.

[pause, section change]

The predicament.
So this is the situation you and I are in: a human being with perception, apparently wandering in samsara. If that is true, why don't I see this samsara? If I honestly asked you right now, are you in samsara — yes, no, or I don't know? Pick one. I picked I don't know when first asked, but now I would say yes. If you said yes, you get a gold star; no, a gold star; I don't know — this is literally what samsara is, the state of confusion.

[pause, section change]

The working definition, then its correction.
Here is the current definition: the condition of a being in which they do not see clearly the state of things as they are. Not seeing the way things are is to be confused, or as the Buddha harshly yet compassionately puts it, wrong view. Imagine you went into space and saw the earth was a sphere and not flat. You would call flat earth the wrong view, even if many people believed it. So would you call a flat earther confused, delusional, or holding the wrong view? Or maybe even correct, in which case I have more bad news for you.

[soft pause] Keep the flat earth for one beat only, because it misleads in exactly the way the essay must then correct. A flat earther can be shown a photo. Your ignorance cannot be shown to you that way, because the instrument doing the looking is what is broken. And one plus one equals two is the wrong image of right view. Right view is not holding the correct sum. As the correction goes: wrong view is taking an appropriated process personally in the act of perceiving, and right view is seeing conditionality stop the appropriation in that moment.

[soft pause] Samsara is the state most people are in: confused about the nature of themselves and existence, holding the wrong view. But this is the illusion. How do I know if I hold the wrong view? You don't. This is ignorance. It is invisible. That is why you can never know your ignorance directly. It is why even when you point out someone's ignorance to them, they look for themselves and they just don't see it. Quite right, because it is not there. Ignorance happens in the very construction of your perception. And because of this, I am in samsara, which is the state of unrealised confusion. Taking this unrealised confusion as the base condition, I then get tangled and go deeper into confusion, yet without realising it.

[soft pause] The claim is that you basically woke up in this confused state, so potentially at no point have you ever not been confused, which is why the claim feels so ridiculous. Especially because billions of other people agree they are not confused. It is like waking up in a maze and seeing everyone you know and love also in that maze without being aware they are in a maze.

[pause, section change]

Why we stay: the sandcastle and the movie.
Which puts anyone who says this in a terrible position. You are in the maze with people you love, who seem happy enough, yet you come along and proclaim they are confused about the entire nature of who they are and what is going on. Doing so undermines the very way one copes with reality, supports loved ones and relates to other people. People need to survive first. The hunger does not stop at food and security. Nicer food, nicer house, the development of the psyche as a strategy to ensure you do not lose those things — over time you build structures on top of ignorance that you depend on in order to get what you want. Therefore you need that fundamental ignorance to sustain your survival, otherwise it would all crumble, like a sandcastle. And it cannot crumble like a sandcastle because your children need that sandcastle to live, so you maintain. Toiling in samsara.

[soft pause] Luckily, as humans we forget incredibly easily. So we forget our fundamental ignorance and focus on enjoying what is in front of us. We can just enjoy the movie. But now our relationship to this fundamental ignorance becomes one of rejection. It is pushed out of vision, necessarily, because the true nature of reality cannot be seen — it would undermine everything your life and perception is built upon.

[soft pause] Two corrections, both load-bearing. First, what would crumble is not food, shelter and love, it is ownership and compulsive maintenance. The arahant still eats and cares; as the texts have it, for the worthy one it is simply the nature of this body to break up, to be laid down — care continues, grasping does not. Second, Dhamma is not reserved for the fed. The first truth is most visible exactly where pressure is greatest.

[pause, section change]

What the texts actually say.
Textually, samsara is sam plus sarati, wandering on. The stock formula in the Anamatagga Samyutta (SN 15.1): "This samsara is without discoverable beginning. A first point is not discerned of beings roaming and wandering on hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving." Ignorance textually is not stupidity. It is specified in the Sammaditthi Sutta (MN 9) and the Vibhanga (SN 12.2): not knowing about suffering, not knowing about the origin of suffering, not knowing about the cessation of suffering, not knowing about the way leading to the cessation of suffering — this is called ignorance.

[soft pause] Now ask, surely there is a first point of ignorance — what is its cause? Ñāṇavīra answers: "Paticcasamuppada depends upon non-seeing of paticcasamuppada... Avijja depends upon avijja... though it is impermanent and constructed, avijja is so stable." In plain terms: not-seeing is itself a determination, a thing other things stand on, so not-seeing stands on not-seeing. That is why it is so stable though nothing in it lasts, and why there is no first point to find — the structure maintains itself. Therefore to see Dhamma is to see dependent arising, so ignorance is non-seeing of dependent arising.

[soft pause] Unpack that: ignorance equals not seeing, active not-seeing of reality as it is, which is impossible to catch because you assume you are seeing reality as it is right now. The hard crux is this: your not-seeing of true reality right now is maintained by your not-seeing of true reality right now. It is obvious in hindsight — your not-seeing ends when you see. So how do you see? This is the subtle point. Ñāṇavīra again: "However much a man may step back from himself to observe himself he cannot help taking avijja with him. There is just as much avijja in the self-observer as there is in the self-observed... In reflexion avijja is the Judge as well as the Accused, and the verdict is always 'Not Guilty'." In plain terms: the very instrument used to discern the truth is flawed. Stepping back does not step outside; it takes the flaw along and acquits itself.

[pause, section change]

Plastic, gold, and the mirror.
Imagine a faulty metal detector at the beach that has never actually detected metal but instead makes noise for plastic. You would say, this is metal — picture of a plastic tub — everyone agrees, look, our metal detector says so. Then someone says, no, actually this is metal. You think the plastic tub is metal, and because of this false first idea you make many further mistakes. You yourself are not doing anything wrong; the detector says the tub is metal, so that is what metal is. It is only when someone puts a gold bar in your hand — is that metal? — that you start to question your plastic tub. You realise, oh, this is metal. Now I can see what I thought was metal was plastic. So someone must introduce you to metal in order for you to know the difference. Now imagine you are so sure of what you are detecting that you laugh at the bare notion it could be anything other.

[soft pause] That is why the Dhamma is called going against the stream (patisotagami): "it gives the outside view inherently unobtainable by unaided reflexion." In plain terms: it is a mirror lent from outside, a description that contradicts your direct evidence, held on trust until your own experience can read itself through it. Essentially, it provides a mirror for ignorance to be seen.

[soft pause] Now imagine if you see reality truly, you can then say, ok, so what I was seeing before wasn't true. Yet how do you explain it? It is like trying to explain confusion. What is the cause of confusion? To be in a state of not seeing clearly. Thus the solution is to see clearly. The blocker is the fundamental not-knowing of one's own confusion. The enabler is the tiny opening caused when one says: are you sure that's true? [pause]

[soft pause] What is the cause of detecting plastic as metal? The detector makes a noise when it sees a plastic tub. You have never seen metal so you cannot discern it from plastic. The very seeing of metal itself transforms your wrong view of what metal is. Now you realise what you were seeing before was plastic — this is metal. Now you see that your metal detector is broken. If someone told you that before you realised it for yourself, you might fight them. As Ñāṇavīra has it: "When it is seen truly it is seen as never having been a thing, only a mis-taking, and seeing removes its condition." In plain terms: what you took for a solid confusion was never a thing at all, only a mis-take, and the seeing is what removes it. Thus one is in a state of ignorance without knowing, investigates and realises one's own ignorance and sees the nature of ignorance, which is fundamentally to see the way things actually are — because when you see the way things actually are, in hindsight you realise you were seeing wrong before. An impossible conclusion to come to without seeing correctly.

[pause, section change]

The whirlpool underneath.
Under the maze image, the canonical image is the whirlpool. Ñāṇananda, with the simile of a whirlpool, explains "samsara on the one hand, and Nibbana on the other, that is to say 'going round', or samsarana, and 'going out', or nissarana." In plain terms: the same water either keeps circling or finds the way out. And the abyss: "Monks, abyss is a synonym for painful bodily feelings." When contacted by pain the uninstructed one sorrows and beats his breast — that is the maze wall, and "the thirst of craving arises in beings because of painful feeling," which is the sandcastle's mortar: delighting in sense pleasures knowing no other way out of pain.

[pause, section change]

The method, honestly held.
A final honesty the essay must keep. Felt certainty is not the test — false memories feel exactly as real as true ones, and past-life regression, however useful as therapy, proves nothing about continuity. What moves this from creed to reasonable belief is checkable detail and the part you can verify tonight: contact comes, tone leans pleasant or painful, grasping either fires or, watched, weakens. Right view in its workable form is exactly that checking, not a proposition believed. And the continuity the texts insist on — rebirth as integral, with no soul-thing carried over, process only — stays as the open question the practice points at, neither affirmed on faith nor denied to close inquiry.

[soft pause] So I ask again: what actually is samsara? In order to answer, don't you need to know what isn't samsara? So perhaps a better question is: have you seen nibbana? [pause]
